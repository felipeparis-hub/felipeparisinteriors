Colour Palettes for a Home in Marbella

How to choose colours that work with Costa del Sol light: warm off-whites, three proven palettes, the 60-30-10 rule and the most common mistakes in Marbella.

By Felipe Paris, interior designer specialising in Roche Bobois in Marbella

Go to my website

When a client shows me photos of their previous home in London, Amsterdam or Paris and says "I want the same colours here", I usually ask them to meet me at the villa around midday. Opening the shutters is enough to see the problem: the light on the Costa del Sol has very little in common with northern European light. It is stronger, whiter at noon, more golden at sunset, and it bounces off terraces, pools and pale floors. A pearl grey that looked elegant in London can look flat or cold here, and a brilliant white can be almost dazzling.

In this article I explain how I choose a colour palette for a home in Marbella, the mistakes I see most often, and how to combine walls, fabrics and furniture so the result works at every hour of the day.

Light first, colour second

Before opening a sample book I always do the same exercise: I look at the house at three different times of day — morning, midday and late afternoon. This is what I pay attention to:

  • The orientation of each room. A south-facing living room gets direct light almost all day; a north-facing one gets cooler, more even light. The same paint looks very different in each.
  • Reflective surfaces. A pool, a pale stone terrace or a polished marble floor send light back inside and tint it. Water adds a bluish note; travertine, a warm one.
  • What you see through the window. If the view is the sea, blue enters the room even if nothing is painted blue. If it is the mountains or a garden, greens and ochres come in.

Once that is clear, I know whether a room needs colours that calm the excess light or colours that warm it up.

Why pure white is rarely the answer

It is the usual temptation in a Mediterranean villa: paint everything white. The problem is that a very cool white under direct southern light tires the eye and makes large spaces feel empty. It also renders every shadow harshly.

I prefer off-whites: whites with a slightly warm base (sand, bone, lime) or, in very sunny rooms, with a touch of grey to soften them. A practical tip: look at the light reflectance value (LRV) that many paint brands list on their colour cards. The higher it is, the more light the wall returns. A living room with a large south-facing window does not need the highest value possible; an interior corridor without windows does.

Three palettes that work on the Costa del Sol

There is no single palette, but three families come up again and again in my projects.

1. Sand, stone and natural wood

The safest palette, and the one that ages best. Warm off-white walls, stone or light wood floors, sofas in linen, beige or taupe, and accents in natural wood and fibres. It works especially well in contemporary villas in Nueva Andalucía or on the Golden Mile, softening straight lines without making them less modern.

2. Contemporary Mediterranean: white, deep blue and terracotta

Here contrast is the star. A very bright base with touches of deep blue (not sky blue, which looks childish in this light) and terracotta or clay. I use it with restraint: a sofa, a pair of armchairs, a rug or a piece of art. If the walls are already white and the view is the sea, two accent colours are enough.

3. Earth tones and muted greens

For homes with gardens, mountain views or areas such as Benahavís and La Zagaleta, I like sage or olive greens, soft ochres and warm browns. They talk to the landscape, and strong light does not wash them out because they are already naturally muted.

How I distribute colour in a room

A rule I often use as a starting point is 60-30-10:

  • 60% for the base colour: walls, floor and large surfaces.
  • 30% for the secondary colour: main sofa, curtains, rug.
  • 10% for accents: cushions, an armchair, ceramics, art.

It is not a law, but it helps avoid two extremes I see a lot: the all-beige house with no focal point, and the house with too many colours fighting each other.

With design furniture such as the Roche Bobois pieces I work with, the sofa usually defines the 30%. Many models can be upholstered in a wide range of fabrics and colours, so the sofa is chosen together with the palette, not afterwards. That is one of the advantages of designing the whole project rather than buying pieces one by one.

Light changes fabrics — and fades them

Something to keep in mind from the start: fabrics in direct sun lose colour over time, and highly saturated shades show it more. In very bright rooms I usually recommend:

  • Keeping the strongest colours for pieces that are not right in front of the window.
  • Using curtains or blinds that filter the light during the harshest hours.
  • Choosing performance fabrics for the most exposed seating.

I went into this in more detail in my guide to seafront villa interior design in Marbella, where I explain which materials best withstand sun, salt and humidity.

The mistakes I see most often

  1. Choosing paint from a small chip in a shop under artificial light. Always paint a large sample on the actual wall and look at it at different times.
  2. Copying the palette of your previous home. What worked in northern light often feels cold here.
  3. Ignoring artificial lighting. In the evening the house lives under lamps. A very cool bulb can turn a warm beige into a sad grey, so choose lighting colour temperature together with the paint.
  4. Painting every room a different colour. In open-plan homes a shared base gives continuity and makes the house feel larger.
  5. Forgetting the outside. The terrace is an extension of the living room; if inside and outside colours do not talk to each other, the transition breaks.

How I work with colour on a project

When I prepare a proposal, colour is not the last step: it is part of the concept from day one. I present it with real fabric and finish samples and, when the project calls for it, with 3D renders of each room so the owner can see the colours in their own light before deciding anything. You can see how a home changes with this process in the before and after projects on my website.

The perfect palette is not the prettiest one in a catalogue, but the one that still looks beautiful at nine in the morning, three in the afternoon and ten at night.
