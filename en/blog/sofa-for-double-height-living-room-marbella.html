Choosing a Sofa for a Double-Height Living Room

How to choose a sofa for a double-height living room in a Marbella villa: scale, shape, back height, fabrics and light. Clear criteria and mistakes to avoid.

By Felipe Paris, interior designer in Marbella

Few things are as striking as walking into a Marbella villa with a double-height living room: ceilings of six or seven metres, a window wall that rises all the way up and Costa del Sol light pouring in. Yet it is one of the spaces I most often see poorly resolved, and the reason is almost always the same: the sofa.

Before becoming an interior designer I worked in real estate on the Costa del Sol and walked through many of these rooms. Then, during five years with Roche Bobois in France, I learned to see a sofa not as a single piece of furniture but as the element that anchors a whole space. In a double-height room that matters even more. Here is how I approach it.

Why a double-height room changes the rules

In a room with a standard ceiling, the eye reads the space horizontally. In a double-height room, the eye travels upwards. If the sofa is small, low and pushed against a wall, the room feels like a huge lobby with one lost piece of furniture. The empty volume weighs more than the furniture.

The aim is not to fill the air but to create a human-scale seating area inside a volume that isn't. The sofa is your main tool for that.

1. Think about scale before the model

The most common mistake is falling for a sofa in a showroom or a photo and transferring it as it is. In a double-height room almost everything looks smaller than it is.

My starting criteria:

  • Floor area, not ceiling height. Height adds a sense of space, but the sofa is sized by the floor and the distance it will be seen from.
  • A composition rather than a single piece. A straight three-seater is rarely enough. An L- or U-shaped modular, or two facing sofas, creates a "room within the room".
  • Tape it out. Before deciding, I mark the outline of the composition on the floor with painter's tape. Seeing the real footprint avoids surprises.

Modular or a sculptural fixed sofa

Modular sofas are my allies in these rooms because they adapt to the floor plan and can be reconfigured over time. A strongly sculptural fixed sofa also works, but usually needs armchairs or a second sofa so it doesn't fall short.

2. Back, seat and depth

A high ceiling suggests a high back. Not necessarily. A low back and a deep seat give a relaxed horizontal silhouette that contrasts beautifully with the vertical space, and it doesn't block the window from the entrance.

What I do recommend:

  • Generous seat depth if the room is truly lived in: films, guests, long afternoons. In holiday villas where people go barefoot, it makes a difference.
  • Loose back cushions so comfort can be adjusted.
  • Test it sitting, not looking. I always ask clients to sit for several minutes.

3. Shape according to the architecture

  1. Long room with a window at the end: a U-shape open towards the view.
  2. Square room: two facing sofas or a curved sofa with armchairs, forming a conversation circle.
  3. Room with a fireplace or art wall: an L-shape facing both the fireplace and the outdoors.
  4. Open plan with kitchen or dining: the sofa floating with its back to the dining area, acting as a divider without walls.

4. Keep it off the wall

Pushing the sofa against the wall is the fastest way to make it look tiny. I prefer it to float, with circulation around it. To make that work, plan floor sockets for floor and reading lamps before any renovation, not after.

5. Fabric, colour and Costa del Sol light

  • Light, natural colours (sand, stone, ecru) amplify the sense of volume; choose easy-care fabrics if there are children, pets or holiday guests.
  • Deeper tones (terracotta, olive, petrol blue) visually ground the sofa and create a cosier zone.
  • Texture over pattern. Bouclé, washed linen or velvet read well from a distance and from the upper gallery.
  • Sun protection. Next to large windows, direct sun can fade fabrics over the years. Curtains or technical blinds protect both sofa and rug.

6. What surrounds the sofa matters too

  • A large rug that holds the whole seating area, with at least the front legs on it.
  • Layered lighting: a large pendant to visually lower the ceiling, plus reading and indirect light.
  • Vertical pieces: tall shelving, large-format art or a sculptural plant that echo the height.
  • The view from above. If there is a gallery, the room is also seen in plan, so an orderly composition pays off.

Mistakes I see often

A sofa that is right in a normal room can look like doll's-house furniture in a double-height one.

  • Buying the sofa before measuring and defining the layout.
  • Choosing from a photo without testing depth and comfort.
  • Scattering small pieces instead of one strong composition.
  • Forgetting floor sockets.
  • Ignoring afternoon sun on the fabric.

How I work on this with clients

I start with the floor plan and how the room will be lived in: how many people, main home or holiday home, children or pets.
